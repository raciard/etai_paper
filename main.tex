\begin{abstract}
Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) route predictions through human-interpretable
concepts, yet both the concept layer and the label space are typically treated as
flat sets, and evaluation reduces to accuracy alone---confusing two warblers
counts the same as confusing a warbler with an albatross.
We ask whether adding hierarchical structure to a supervised CBM can improve
interpretability and error quality on CUB-200-2011 (200 bird species), even when
raw accuracy does not increase.
We introduce structure on two axes.
On the \emph{concept} side, we (i)~predict each concept from the anatomical-part
crop it describes, breaking the \emph{prediction circularity} that lets a
whole-image encoder infer a concept from class-diagnostic shortcuts rather than
local evidence, and (ii)~organise color/pattern attributes in a coarse-to-fine
hierarchy.
On the \emph{label} side, we map the 200 species onto a WordNet taxonomy,
derive a Lowest-Common-Ancestor (LCA) severity metric, and apply a post-hoc
decision rule that minimises expected severity without retraining.
With the backbone fixed across all variants, an unconstrained black-box still
beats every concept-based model on both accuracy and severity, confirming the
known cost of the bottleneck.
Within that budget the concept-space changes do not raise accuracy---and
slightly increase severity---but they make the bottleneck faithful: concept-level
evaluation shows that the flat model's higher apparent concept scores partly
stem from the same class-correlated shortcuts that inflate its species accuracy,
rather than from genuinely grounded evidence.
The label-space rule, meanwhile, lowers severity at negligible accuracy cost for
most concept-based variants, provided the classifier's posterior is reliable
enough for a severity-weighted re-ranking to trust in the first place.
Taken together, the two forms of hierarchy benefit error quality and
interpretability rather than accuracy, and they are best combined.
\end{abstract}
