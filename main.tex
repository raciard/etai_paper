\begin{abstract}
Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) route a prediction through a layer of
human-interpretable concepts, but both the concept layer and the label space
are usually treated as flat, unstructured sets, and evaluation reduces to
accuracy---so a mistake between two closely related birds is scored exactly like
a mistake between two unrelated ones. We study whether injecting hierarchical
structure into a supervised CBM improves interpretability and the semantic
quality of its errors on fine-grained bird recognition (CUB-200-2011),
independently of raw accuracy. We add structure along two axes. In the
\emph{concept space} we (i) predict each concept from the anatomical part it
names, breaking the \emph{prediction circularity} by which a whole-image concept
predictor can infer a concept from global, class-correlated shortcuts rather than
from its own visual evidence, and (ii) give color/pattern attributes a
coarse-to-fine structure. In the \emph{label space} we map the 200 species onto a
WordNet taxonomy that yields a Lowest-Common-Ancestor (LCA) mistake-severity
metric and a post-hoc, retraining-free decision rule that minimizes expected
severity instead of taking the arg\,max. Holding the feature extractor fixed
across all variants, we find that the concept-space interventions do not raise
accuracy---and slightly increase average severity---while making the bottleneck
faithful and more interpretable, whereas the calibrated label-space decision rule
lowers mistake severity at negligible accuracy cost. The benefit of this added
structure thus concentrates in error quality and interpretability rather than in
accuracy.
\end{abstract}
