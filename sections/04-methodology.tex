\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
We build the two axes of structure from Section~\ref{sec:research-gaps} on one
shared setup, so differences are attributable to the bottleneck design, not
backbone, split, or training budget.

\textbf{Setup.} We use CUB-200-2011~\cite{wah2011caltech} (200 species,
bounding-box crops). Concepts are class-level attributes obtained with
\citet{koh2020concept}'s procedure: instance-level CUB attributes are
majority-voted to the class level and rare ones discarded, leaving $109$
concepts. Every variant uses the same ConvNeXt-Tiny~\cite{liu2022convnet}
backbone, trained from scratch. As an accuracy upper bound we also train a
black-box (ConvNeXt-Tiny with a linear head straight to the 200 species, no
bottleneck). All concept-based variants use a linear classifier from the
concept vector to the species.

\textbf{Flat CBM.} One ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder predicts all $109$ concepts from
the whole image, with no part localisation.

\textbf{Part-conditioned CBM.} Concepts predicted from the whole image can be
inferred through class-correlated shortcuts instead of real evidence. We
localise each concept's evidence before predicting it. A frozen Keypoint
R-CNN detects 12 anatomical parts and their visibility,
producing one crop per part plus a global crop. ConvNeXt-Tiny backbones
encode these crops (shared across parts, separate for the global crop), and
each attribute is predicted only from the crop of the part it names---
\emph{wing color} only sees the wing crop. Undetected parts get a learned
``absent'' embedding. Figure~\ref{fig:part-crops} shows this on a test image.

\textbf{Concept consistency test.} To check that part conditioning actually
grounds a concept in the region it names, we later intervene on that region
directly. On $100$ CUB-200-2011 test images with an unambiguous ground-truth
crown colour, we use a generative image-editing model (Gemini~2.5~Flash~Image)
to change \emph{only} the crown colour, keeping pose, camera angle,
background, and every other visible attribute fixed, and re-run each concept
encoder on the edited image (Figure~\ref{fig:crown-edit-example}). We report
the fraction of edited images whose predicted crown colour changes at all,
and the fraction landing on the intended target colour.

\textbf{Coarse-to-fine concepts.} $22$ of the $28$ attribute groups---every
colour and pattern group---get an extra coarse level, built from CUB's actual
attribute vocabulary: colour groups map their fine values onto $4$ families
(warm / cool / neutral / iridescent), pattern groups onto $2$ categories
(uniform / patterned). For these groups, a small linear head predicts the
coarse family from the same part (or global) embedding the fine head would
otherwise use directly; its softmax posterior is concatenated to that
embedding, and a second linear head predicts the fine value from the
concatenation, so the fine prediction is conditioned on the coarse one rather
than just given it as an extra feature. Both heads train jointly: the coarse
head against an auxiliary cross-entropy loss, added to the fine head's usual
loss, using the coarse family of whichever fine value is confidently
annotated as ground truth. The remaining groups (shape, size, length) have no
shared vocabulary to build a coarse level from, and keep a single flat head.

\textbf{Independent vs.\ sequential.} \citet{koh2020concept} train the species
classifier three ways: \emph{independent} (fit on ground-truth concepts),
\emph{sequential} (fit on the encoder's own predicted concepts), and
\emph{joint} (fit on both). We test only the first two, since joint training
needs an extensive hyperparameter search to balance the two losses and a more
complex training schedule. So we train the linear species classifier on
ground-truth concept labels (independent) and on the concept encoder's own
predicted probabilities (sequential), so it can learn to compensate for the
encoder's errors. Both are evaluated on the real predicted concepts.

\textbf{Label-space hierarchy and mistake severity.} We map the 200 species
onto WordNet (species\,$\to$\,genus\,$\to$\,family\,$\to\cdots$)
~\cite{miller1995wordnet}, giving a tree $\mathcal{T}$. For nodes $a,b$,
$\mathrm{LCA}(a,b)$ is their lowest common ancestor, and $h(v)$ is the height
of $v$ (edges to its deepest leaf, so $h=0$ for leaves). Following
\citet{bertinetto2020making}, the severity of predicting $\hat{y}$ when the
truth is $y$ is
\begin{equation}
  s(y,\hat{y}) \;=\; h\!\left(\mathrm{LCA}(y,\hat{y})\right),
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ $0$ if correct, $1$ if same genus, $2$ if same family, and so on. We
report the mean over the test set:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Sev} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} s\!\left(y_i,\hat{y}_i\right).
  \label{eq:sev}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Severity-aware decision (CRM).} Let $p(c\mid x)$ be the classifier's
posterior over the $C{=}200$ classes. Instead of arg\,max, CRM
~\cite{karthik2021nocost} picks the class of minimum expected severity:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{CRM}}
  \;=\; \arg\min_{k\in\{1,\dots,C\}} \sum_{c=1}^{C} s(k,c)\,p(c\mid x).
  \label{eq:crm}
\end{equation}
It is applied post-hoc to an already-trained classifier's posterior, with no
retraining and no extra parameters.

\textbf{Metrics.} At the species level: top-1 accuracy and the severity of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sev} (lower is better). At the concept level: binary accuracy
and macro-$F_1$ over the $109$ attributes for the concept encoder alone, to
check whether a higher concept score reflects grounded evidence or a
shortcut.
