\section{Introduction}
Fine-grained visual recognition---telling apart 200 visually similar bird
species---is a demanding setting in which \emph{why} a model made a decision is
often as important as the decision itself. Concept Bottleneck Models
(CBMs)~\cite{koh2020concept} address this by forcing the prediction to pass
through an intermediate layer of human-interpretable concepts: an image is first
mapped to a vector of concepts (the class-level bird attributes of CUB-200-2011%
~\cite{wah2011caltech} selected following \citet{koh2020concept}, directly
supervised), and only then to a species. This exposes concept-level explanations
and enables test-time intervention on individual concepts.

The standard CBM, however, leaves two problems open. First, the concepts are
predicted from the \emph{whole} image: because concepts and classes are correlated
in the data, a whole-image predictor can infer a concept such as \emph{wing color}
from features that are diagnostic of the \emph{species} rather than of the wing.
The concept vector then leaks class information and the explanation becomes
\emph{circular}---the concept is ``predicted'' from a representation that already
encodes the answer, so its activation does not faithfully reflect the local
evidence it names, and concept interventions no longer behave as intended.
Second, the label space is flat and the model is judged by accuracy alone, which
treats every mistake as equally bad: confusing two warblers counts the same as
confusing a warbler with an albatross. Our contribution is a study that (i)
predicts each concept from a crop of the part it describes, localized by
\emph{Keypoint R-CNN}, to break this circularity and force every concept to be
grounded in its own evidence; (ii) adds a coarse-to-fine structure to
color/pattern concepts; (iii) equips the label space with a WordNet-based severity
metric and a post-hoc decision rule; and (iv) analyses the resulting accuracy,
severity and interpretability trade-offs on CUB-200-2011.
